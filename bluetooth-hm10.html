Integrating the HM-10

Swapping the kit's classic-Bluetooth HC-06 for a BLE HM-10 in the shield's XBee socket. The sketches need no changes at all — but the bundled Android app stops working the moment you do it.

What the kit ships

The manual calls it a "Bluetooth XBee module HC-06" (pp. 30–31): an HC-06 on an XBee-format carrier that plugs into a socket on the balance shield, paired from the phone with PIN 1234 (p. 117). The socket is a 3.3 V system and sits on the UNO's hardware UART, D0/D1.

The one thing that breaks

Balance_car_Keyes.apk will not work with an HM-10

The bundled app speaks classic Bluetooth SPP — that is what the PIN-1234 pairing flow is. The HM-10 is BLE 4.0 (CC2541) and does not implement SPP at all. The app will not find the module, let alone connect to it. Lessons 11, 12 and 13 lose their control path until you replace the app.

Why the sketches don't care

Every Bluetooth lesson does exactly two things: Serial.begin(9600) and Serial.read(). The module is a transparent UART pipe either way, and the HM-10's default baud is 9600 as well. Lessons 05, 11, 12 and 13 compile and run untouched.

Wiring

Power and ground go straight across. The two data lines cross — that is the whole diagram, and the one thing worth getting right before anything else:

A straight-through mistake looks like a dead module, not a wiring fault

Swap the two data lines and nothing errors: the module still powers up, still advertises, and your phone still connects. You simply never reach the Arduino. Because the BLE side looks perfectly healthy, this is the slowest fault on the page to diagnose — check the crossover first, not last.

The socket is a 3.3 V system, which suits the HM-10 natively. Do not feed a bare module from the 5 V rail.

Why receiving is the direction that conflicts

The XBee socket sits on the same hardware UART as the USB-serial chip — and the two directions are not symmetric:

So sending telemetry back to the phone costs you nothing: the serial monitor just shows a copy of whatever the car tells the app, which is genuinely useful while debugging. Receiving is the half with two drivers on one wire.

The shield already has the answer

Per the manual (pp. 113 and 262) the balance shield carries a Bluetooth switch. You do not have to pull the module: flip that switch off while the USB cable is in, and on once it is out. The documented sequence is upload → unplug USB → power switch on → Bluetooth switch on.

Which gives one genuinely useful trick: with the Bluetooth switch off, the serial monitor is a stand-in for the phone. Type the same single characters — F, B, L, R, S — and you can exercise the entire command protocol with no radio in the picture at all. Prove the firmware there first, then turn the radio on and you are only debugging the BLE link.

The BLE side

The HM-10 exposes a transparent serial pipe as a single GATT characteristic — write to it to send bytes to the Arduino, subscribe to notifications to receive them:

UUIDUse
Service0000ffe0-0000-1000-8000-00805f9b34fbthe transparent UART service
Characteristic0000ffe1-0000-1000-8000-00805f9b34fbboth write and notify

One characteristic, not two

Unlike the Nordic-style UART service — which splits TX and RX into separate characteristics — the HM-10 uses FFE1 for both directions. Code written against a Nordic UART will not port across unchanged.

What to send it

The protocol is single ASCII characters, read one at a time. Taken from the sketches themselves, so this is what the firmware actually accepts:

No terminators, no framing, no checksum — one byte in, action out. Anything that can write a single character to FFE1 can drive the car.

The return path

Both directions run over that one FFE1 characteristic, but by two different mechanisms: you write to it to reach the Arduino, and the Arduino reaches you through notifications on the same characteristic. A client that only writes will drive the car perfectly and never see a single reply.

Enable notifications, or RX silently does nothing

Subscribing — writing the CCCD descriptor 0x2902, usually wrapped as startNotifications() or a "subscribe" toggle — is what turns the return path on. Skipping it is the classic half-working symptom: commands land, telemetry never arrives, and nothing anywhere reports an error.

On the wire it is still an ordinary UART, so the module's TX goes to the board's RX and its RX to the board's TX. Get that crossover wrong and you lose one direction while the other keeps working — see below.

The replies have no terminator

It is Serial.print(angle), not println — lessons 12 and 13 send the digits and nothing else: no newline, no length, no delimiter. The module packetises whatever happens to be in its UART buffer, so the client receives unframed fragments with no way to tell where one value ends and the next begins. Either treat one notification as one value, or change those calls to Serial.println() and make the return path trivial to parse. Lesson 11's echo is the one that already uses println.

Four things that will catch you

1 · Lesson 12 turns any unknown byte into the balance angle

Its parser ends with an else branch that assigns the raw received byte straight into angle0 — the mechanical balance setpoint. Send a command with a trailing line ending and the car reads 10 (LF) or 13 (CR) as "lean ten degrees", and lurches. Most BLE terminal apps append a line ending by default. Send bare single characters with nothing after them. Lessons 11 and 13 have no such branch.

2 · Most "HM-10" modules on sale are clones

CC41-A and MLT-BT05 boards are sold as HM-10 and behave like one for plain data, but their AT command set differs: genuine HM-10 takes AT+NAMEmycar with no CR/LF, clones take AT+NAME=mycar with CR/LF. Send a bare AT and see what comes back before assuming either.

3 · Get DOUT/DIN the right way round

An HM-10 usually ships as a bare 4–6 pin breakout, not an XBee carrier — so unless you have an XBee-format one, you are jumpering into the socket. The module's TX goes to the board's RX and vice versa; a straight-through wiring is silent, not broken-looking, which makes it the slowest fault to spot.

4 · You still have to isolate it before flashing

Swapping the radio changes nothing about the shared UART. Turn the shield's Bluetooth switch off before uploading — bootloader traffic on D0 is exactly the collision described above — or it still fails with avrdude: stk500_getsync(): not in sync. The serial monitor and the phone still cannot both be sending.

Replacing the app

Since the protocol is single characters over a transparent pipe, anything that speaks BLE GATT will do:

  • A generic BLE terminal — nRF Connect, LightBlue, or any "BLE serial" app. Connect, find FFE1, send F. Good enough to prove the swap works before building anything.
  • A Web Bluetooth page — a single HTML file with drive buttons and the ten tuning keys, opened in Chrome on a phone or laptop, no install and no APK. It fits this repo's existing shape, and it is the option that also gets you iOS via a browser that supports it.

The tuning keys are the strongest argument for building the page rather than using a generic terminal: lesson 12 is meant to be used with a thumb on each gain while the car is running, and that is painful in a raw hex terminal.
